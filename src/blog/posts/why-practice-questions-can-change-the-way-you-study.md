Reading a chapter feels like studying. Answering a question about it is studying. The difference matters more than most students realize — especially when the exam is a CBT with a timer running.

## The Problem with Passive Studying

Reading gives you information. Practice reveals what you actually *know*.

When you re-read your notes, the material feels familiar — and familiarity feels like mastery. Psychologists call this the **fluency illusion**, and it's the reason students walk out of exams saying "I read everything, but I blanked." You knew the page. You didn't know the answer.

Practice questions break that illusion immediately. The moment you have to produce an answer — not recognize one — you find out the truth.

## What Practice Questions Actually Do

A good practice session does three things reading never can:

- **Tests retrieval, not recognition.** Pulling an answer out of your memory strengthens it far more than seeing it again on a page. Researchers call this the *testing effect* — being tested on material produces better long-term retention than re-studying it.
- **Exposes your weak spots early.** Every wrong answer is a free diagnostic. It tells you exactly which topic to fix — while there's still time to fix it.
- **Trains you for the real format.** Nigerian exams are increasingly computer-based. Practicing timed MCQs on a screen is a completely different skill from highlighting a textbook.

## How to Practice Well

Not all practice is equal. These habits separate students who improve from students who just collect questions:

1. **Answer before you peek.** Cover the options, recall the answer, then check. If you read the options first, you're recognizing — not retrieving.
2. **Review every wrong answer.** The explanation matters more than the score. One misunderstood concept can cost you five questions in the real exam.
3. **Space it out.** Practicing a topic once and forgetting it doesn't help. The questions should come back — harder each time, right before you'd forget them. (We wrote about [the science behind this](/blog/science-of-spaced-repetition).)
4. **Simulate exam conditions.** Timed, no notes, one sitting. The pressure is part of the training.
