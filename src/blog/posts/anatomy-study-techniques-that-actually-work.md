Anatomy is where a lot of first-year med students hit the wall. The volume is brutal, the structures all blur together, and the exam doesn't ask "did you read Gray's" — it asks "where does the ulnar nerve run?"

The good news: anatomy isn't hard because of the content. It's hard because most people study it the way they'd study an essay. These are the techniques that actually hold up in the dissection hall and the exam hall.

## 1. Draw it from memory — don't just look at it

The single most effective anatomy habit costs nothing: after studying a structure, close the atlas and **draw it from memory**. Labels, relations, everything you remember. Then compare.

It will be embarrassingly wrong the first time. That's the point — the wrongness shows you exactly what you didn't retain, which re-reading would have hidden. Five minutes of bad drawing beats thirty minutes of staring at a labeled diagram you think you know.

## 2. Learn relations, not locations

Examiners rarely ask "what is the axillary artery?" They ask "what passes posterior to it?" or "what gets injured in a fracture of the surgical neck?"

Anatomy that sticks is anatomy in context. When you learn a structure, always attach it to its neighbors: what runs with it, what's superficial to it, what it supplies. You'll remember one nerve through three relationships long before you remember it in isolation.

## 3. Use mnemonics — but only for raw lists

Mnemonics earn their keep for pure memorization: cranial nerves, carpal bones, contents of the femoral triangle. A dirty mnemonic you'll never forget beats a clean list you'll never retain.

But don't mnemonic what you can understand. "Some Lovers Try Positions" works because carpal bones have no story. The brachial plexus, on the other hand, has a real structure — roots, trunks, divisions, cords, branches. Learn the *architecture* and the branches make sense instead of needing a mnemonic per branch.

## 4. Test yourself before you feel ready

This is the one most students skip because it feels bad. Waiting until you "know it" to test yourself is backwards — **retrieval practice is what builds the memory**, not the reward for having built it. Struggling to recall the branches of the celiac trunk is literally the exercise that makes you remember them.

Timed questions add a second benefit: professional exams are CBTs, and answering under a clock is a separate skill from knowing the content. Practice questions from your own lecture material — not a random question bank — keep you aligned with what your lecturers actually ask.

## 5. Explain it out loud to someone who didn't study it

If you can teach the fascial compartments of the thigh to a friend in five minutes — in your own words, no notes — you know it. If you stall, you just found your weak spot for free.

## 6. Never let a diagram beat you

There's a specific frustration every med student knows: staring at a labeled cross-section, unable to tell what plane you're even looking at. Don't push through it — get it explained.

## 7. Review on a schedule, not on a panic

Anatomy's volume is why it punishes cramming specifically — there is simply too much to re-cover the night before. What survives to exam day is what you reviewed at widening intervals: day one, day three, week one, week two.

## The Bottom Line

Anatomy rewards active techniques and punishes passive ones. Draw, relate, test, teach — and review on a schedule instead of a panic. Do those consistently and the dissection hall stops being a place you survive and becomes where your memory actually gets built.
